■ StartBusiness24 Guide — IP Value & Collateral

What Your IP Is Worth — and How Owners Borrow Against It

"How much is a trademark worth?" The honest answer disappoints people: the registration certificate itself is worth very little. Value is built by the business around it. This guide covers where IP value actually comes from, the three ways professionals estimate it, and — once value exists — how owners pledge IP as collateral, and what that really puts at risk.

This guide is education, not legal advice — IP filings are a place where a patent/trademark attorney earns their fee. The same goes double for loan documents that pledge your brand.

Part A — How IP gets value

Trademarks gain value from use in commerce. A mark is worth what the business under it has built: sales made under the brand, customers who recognize it and come back, and any licensing or royalty income the brand earns when others pay to use it. A name nobody has bought from yet has paperwork, not value — the filing receipt is the seed, the revenue is the tree.

Patents gain value from exclusion. A patent's core right is the power to stop others from making, using, or selling the invention. That exclusion turns into money three ways: the margin on products only you may sell, licensing royalties from companies that pay to use it, and resale — selling the patent itself. A patent covering something nobody wants excludes competitors from a market that does not exist, and is worth about that much.

The three ways professionals estimate it

  • Cost approach — what it cost to create. Adds up what was spent developing and filing the IP. Treat it as a floor, never a value: spending heavily to build something nobody buys does not make it worth what it cost.
  • Market approach — what similar IP traded for. Looks at sales and license deals for comparable marks or patents. Honest problem for young IP: there is rarely a true comparable, so this method often has little to stand on.
  • Income approach — the earnings the IP drives. Estimates the income the IP produces over its remaining life. Its common form is called relief-from-royalty: the value of a brand is roughly what you would have to pay to rent an equivalent brand if you did not own yours.

The honest line to remember: most young IP is worth little on paper until it produces revenue — value follows the business, not the filing receipt. Formal appraisals exist, they cost real money, and two appraisers can land far apart on the same asset. Any person who quotes you a firm value for a young trademark or patent without seeing revenue records is selling you a number.

Part B — Collateralizing it: pledging IP to a lender

When IP does carry value, a lender can take a security interest in it — the same basic machinery used for equipment or receivables. Done properly, the pledge is built in three layers:

  • 1. The security agreement. Your loan documents grant the lender a security interest in the IP, often inside a broader lien on the business's assets — lawyers file IP under a category called "general intangibles." This agreement is the contract that creates the lender's rights.
  • 2. The UCC-1 financing statement, filed with the state. Under UCC Article 9 — the state-law system that governs secured lending against business assets — the lender files a financing statement (the UCC-1) with the state's central filing office, usually the Secretary of State. That filing puts the claim on public record where other lenders and buyers can find it.
  • 3. Recording the interest with the USPTO. For patents and registered trademarks, the lender also records its interest in the USPTO's Assignment Center — the federal record of ownership documents for patents and trademarks — so the claim shows in both the state record and the federal record. The recording fees on the USPTO's schedule are small: $40 for the first trademark covered by a document and $25 for each additional mark; patent documents record at no charge when submitted electronically ($54 when they are not).

What lenders actually look for

  • Revenue history under the brand — sales records beat projections, every time.
  • Licensing income with paperwork — signed licenses and royalties actually received.
  • Remaining life — a patent with two years left of its 20-year term is weak collateral next to one with fifteen.
  • Clean, enforceable rights — registrations current, maintenance filings paid, ownership chain clear, no disputes hanging over the asset.
  • Cash flow that services the loan — in most real deals, IP supports the loan; the business's cash flow is still the main reason it gets made.

Where does pledged IP show up in the real world? In asset-based lending, where loan size is set against the value of business assets — IP among them — and in loans backed by the U.S. Small Business Administration (SBA), where lenders are expected to take liens on the borrower's available business assets, a package that can include the business's IP. Read that carefully: it means IP can be part of the collateral story. Approval, amounts, and terms always depend on the lender's judgment and your eligibility — if you qualify, IP strengthens an application; it never replaces one.

The risks, stated plainly

Lenders discount IP hard. Expect credit for a fraction of any appraised value — and for young IP, expect the fraction to be at or near zero. Default can cost you the brand itself: the pledged trademark or patent is exactly what the lender can take and sell. Valuation costs money before any loan exists — an appraisal is an expense, not a loan approval. And a trademark lives only while it is used: stop using the mark, or let a patent's maintenance fees lapse, and the registration and the collateral can die together.
