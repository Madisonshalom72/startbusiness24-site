How to Patent an Idea — Starting With the Honest Rule

The honest rule comes first: an idea alone can't be patented — an invention can. A patent protects something built and fully described, not the thought in your head. Here is how an idea becomes an invention on paper, the three tests the USPTO applies, the provisional shortcut, the real fees off the official schedule, and the disclosure clock that quietly ends applications before they start.

This guide is education, not legal advice — IP filings are a place where a patent/trademark attorney earns their fee. The USPTO says the same thing in its own filing guide: a patent application "is a complex legal document, best prepared by one trained to prepare such documents."

The three tests every invention faces

A patent is a property right granted by the USPTO: for a limited time, it gives the owner the right to exclude others from making, using, selling, or importing the invention. Before the USPTO grants one, an examiner tests the invention against the patent statute's three basic requirements:

  • Useful. The invention does something real — it has a specific, practical purpose, and it works as described.
  • Novel. It is new: not already patented, described in a publication, publicly used, on sale, or otherwise out in the world before your filing date — anywhere, not just in the United States.
  • Non-obvious. It is not an obvious next step to a person with ordinary skill in the field. Simply combining two known things in the predictable way usually fails this test — it is where strong applications most often die.

There is a fourth, quieter requirement: your application must describe the invention in full, clear terms, claims included, so a skilled person could make and use it. An undocumented idea in your head fails before the three tests even start.

The three kinds of patent

  • Utility patent — how it works. For anyone who invents or discovers a new and useful process, machine, article of manufacture, or composition of matter, or a new and useful improvement to one. This is the main road: most patent applications the USPTO receives are utility applications.
  • Design patent — how it looks. For a new, original, and ornamental design for an article of manufacture. It protects appearance only, never function.
  • Plant patent — a new plant. For anyone who invents or discovers and asexually reproduces a distinct and new variety of plant.

The steps

  • 1. Document the invention. Keep dated records as you build: what it is, how it works, sketches and drawings, versions, tests, and who saw it, when. If the invention is ever questioned — by an examiner, a partner, or a court — the dated paper trail is the evidence that it is yours and when you had it.
  • 2. Search before you spend. Search existing patents and published applications for anything that already describes your invention ("prior art"). Work the USPTO's own method first — its multi-step U.S. patent search strategy — and use Google Patents as a second search tool. Search tools only: they file nothing and decide nothing. Finding your invention already described is painful, but learning it in a free search is the cheapest version of that lesson.
  • 3. Provisional application — buy 12 months. A provisional application is a quick, lower-cost way to plant an early U.S. filing date. The USPTO does not examine it, it is automatically abandoned 12 months after its filing date, and during those 12 months you may mark the invention "Patent Pending." The filing fee on the official USPTO schedule is $325 — $130 for applicants who qualify as small entities, $65 for micro entities. Two cautions: a provisional only protects what it actually describes, so a thin write-up plants a thin flag; and it never turns into a patent by itself — step 4 is not optional.
  • 4. Nonprovisional application — the real one, within 12 months. To keep the provisional's filing date, the full nonprovisional application must be filed during the provisional's 12-month window. It is filed electronically in Patent Center, the USPTO's online filing system (filing on paper adds a $400 non-electronic filing fee — $200 for small and micro entities). The government's filing, search, and examination fees from the official schedule total $2,000 — $800 for small entities, $400 for micro entities (filing $350 / $140 / $70, search $770 / $308 / $154, examination $880 / $352 / $176). Small entity status cuts most patent fees by 60% and micro entity status by 80% — claim a status only if you actually qualify for it; the USPTO treats a false claim as a serious problem.
  • 5. Examination, issue, and upkeep. A patent examiner tests your claims against the three requirements and the prior art. Expect at least one office action — a written round of objections or rejections that you answer, often by narrowing the claims — and expect the back-and-forth to take real time: the USPTO receives more than 600,000 patent applications a year, and from filing to an issued patent commonly takes years, not months. If the application is allowed, an issue fee is due ($1,290; small entity $516; micro entity $258). A utility patent runs 20 years from its filing date, and keeping it in force costs maintenance fees at 3.5, 7.5, and 11.5 years — on the current schedule, $2,150, $4,040, and $8,280 (small entity $860 / $1,616 / $3,312; micro entity $430 / $808 / $1,656). Fees on this page come from the official USPTO fee schedule; the USPTO adjusts fees over time, so confirm the current schedule before filing. The USPTO's own walkthrough: Nonprovisional (Utility) Patent Application Filing Guide.

The disclosure clock: read this before you show anyone

Selling, publishing, demoing, or posting your invention in public starts a clock. U.S. law gives you a one-year grace period for your own disclosures: under 35 U.S.C. § 102(b)(1), as the USPTO's examination manual (§ 2153) explains it, a disclosure made by you — or by someone who got the invention from you — one year or less before your filing date does not count as prior art against you. Know the two traps. First, the grace period covers only inventor-originated disclosures; it does nothing about a stranger's independent publication. Second, most other countries run stricter rules with no grace period at all — a public reveal before filing can end foreign patent rights the day it happens. The safe sequence is the simple one: file first (a provisional counts), then show the world.

Protect the brand around the invention

A patent covers the invention; a trademark covers the name customers will ask for. Most product businesses end up needing both — and the business itself should exist before either filing names an owner.
